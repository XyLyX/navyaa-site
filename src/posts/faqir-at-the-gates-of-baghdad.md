*By Xylyx | 24 November 2026*

Every year, when the full moon of Kartik rises, Sikhs around the world mark Gurpurab, the festival of the Guru. The Guru in question is Nanak, born in 1469 at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, the town now called Nankana Sahib in Pakistan. I have lived in the Gulf since 2007, and I have long been struck by how this festival, among the most joyful in the Indian calendar, begins so quietly: with a text read aloud, without pause, for two days.

That reading, the Akhand Path, is the spine of the observance. Around it come the Nagar Kirtan processions led by the Panj Pyare with the saffron flag, the pre-dawn pheris of hymn-singing through neighbourhoods, lamps on gurdwara walls, and above all the langar, the free vegetarian meal in which everyone sits on the same floor, whatever their caste, religion or means. For a festival about a founder, there is remarkably little ceremony about the founder himself. The emphasis falls on what he asked people to do: to remember, to work honestly, and to share.

A small historical footnote I find honest rather than awkward: the earliest Janamsakhi tradition places Nanak's birth in the spring month of Vaisakh, and it is the later tradition that settled on Kartik Poornima. The community chose the date it wanted to gather on. That says something about how living faiths work.

## A teacher who walked

Between roughly 1500 and 1524, according to tradition, Nanak made four long journeys, the Udasis, with his companion Bhai Mardana, a Muslim musician who played the rabab while Nanak sang. The figure usually quoted is some 28,000 kilometres on foot, which should be read as a devotional estimate rather than a surveyed distance. The biographical accounts, the Janamsakhis, were written decades later, and they are better read as teaching literature than as itineraries. But as teaching they are superb, because each story makes an argument through an action, not a doctrine.

- **Haridwar, the water thrown west.** Pilgrims fling water east for their ancestors. Nanak throws it the other way, to irrigate his fields in Punjab. If mine can't travel that far, he asks, how will yours? *Ritual without compassion is theatre.*
- **Hassan Abdal, the boulder.** A thirsty Mardana is refused water by a hilltop ascetic; a spring rises below, the hilltop one dries, and a rolled boulder is stopped by a palm. *Hoarding blessings makes you dry.*
- **Saidpur, milk and blood.** He declines a rich man's feast for a poor carpenter's coarse bread, and presses both. *Honest earning is clean; exploitation leaves a stain.*
- **Multan, the bowl and the petal.** The city's saints send a bowl of milk filled to the brim: there is no room. He floats a jasmine petal on it. *Real spirituality adds fragrance; it doesn't compete for space.*
- **Mecca, the feet.** Rebuked for sleeping with his feet toward the Kaaba, he asks to have them turned toward wherever God is not. *The sacred cannot be confined to a direction.*

## West of the Indus

The westward journey is the one that interests me most, because it is where the story leaves a world that was already his and walks into a world that was not. Tradition has him dressed as a faqir in blue, with a staff, a prayer mat and a water pot, travelling by sea to Arabia, speaking with scholars and Sufis in Mecca and Medina, and then going north to Baghdad, then a great seat of learning. He camps outside the walls. At dawn, Mardana plays and Nanak sings, and a city that did not much care for public music takes notice.

> He is remembered in Baghdad not as a founder but as a guest, which is a far better kind of evidence.

It is here that the Sikh tradition, which usually has to rely on texts written later, receives something rarer: a physical witness.

## The stone that speaks

For centuries, in the Karkh district of Baghdad, next to the tomb of a local Sufi called Bahlol Dana, stood a small shrine known as the Baba Nanak shrine, looked after by Muslim custodians. Inside was an inscription in Ottoman Turkish written in Arabic script. The photographs below, which I have cleaned and cropped from poor-quality scans, show it as it hung on the wall.

![The shrine wall in Baghdad, with the four-line Turkish inscription at centre and a framed plaque at top right](/images/uploads/baghdad-shrine-wall-inscriptions.jpg)

*Fig. 1. The shrine wall. The whitewashed centre carries the four-line Turkish verse; a second, framed plaque with similar text sits at top right, and the date is circled below it. Photograph of a printed plate; original source to be credited.*

The text is a short poem. In my paraphrase, not a verbatim translation: it declares that God has granted a wish, that a new building has been raised for Baba Nanak the dervish, that seven holy men helped with the work, and that a devoted follower caused fresh water to well up from the earth. The last line is a chronogram, a date hidden in the numerical values of the letters.

![Close-up of the four-line Ottoman Turkish inscription](/images/uploads/baghdad-turkish-inscription.jpg)

*Fig. 2. The four lines in close-up, with the date written below. The figure I can make out is 927 AH (about 1520–21 CE).*

A second text, in Arabic, opens with the words "Qāl Nānak Faqīr", Nanak the faqir said, and goes on in verse and then in prose. I am reading a low-resolution photograph, so treat my reading as provisional: the prose seems to refer to the lodge associated with Bahlol, and it carries a date that looks like 917 AH (about 1511–12 CE).

![Arabic panel opening with the words Qal Nanak Faqir](/images/uploads/baghdad-arabic-panel.jpg)

*Fig. 3. The Arabic panel. It opens "Qāl Nānak Faqīr", and carries what looks like a date of 917 AH. Reading from a degraded plate; check against a published translation.*

There is also a carved slab of seven cartouche lines, which circulates under the label "stone slab from Guru Nanak's shrine". I haven't been able to confirm its text, so I show it as a documented object rather than a quotation.

![Carved stone slab with seven cartouche lines of Arabic script](/images/uploads/baghdad-carved-slab.jpg)

*Fig. 4. Carved slab attributed to the shrine. Text unverified by me.*

## Two dates, one question

The two dates, 917 and 927 AH, sit a decade apart, and they don't describe the same year. Popular accounts, including the text I started from, tend to smooth this over by saying both "align" with the Fourth Udasi. They don't: 1511 is earlier than the usual 1518–21 window. One possibility, and I stress that it is my speculation, is that one date marks a visit and the other a building. Another is that the Sikh chronology of the Udasis is simply looser than the clarity we want from it. Either way, the honest position is that the inscriptions confirm that someone called Baba Nanak, faqir, was remembered in Baghdad early enough to be commemorated in stone. They do not settle a precise year.

- **Documented.** Inscriptions naming "Baba Nanak" as a faqir existed in a Baghdad shrine next to Bahlol Dana's tomb, and were photographed in the early twentieth century.
- **Tradition.** The sea route, the camp outside the walls, Mardana's rabab, the debate on countless worlds, the sweet-water well. Beloved, early, but written later.
- **Unsettled.** The exact year, the identity of the "seven" helpers, the precise wording of the Arabic panel, and the fate of the original plaque.

One correction worth making, because it keeps appearing: the Sufi in the story is Bahlol Dana, not Pir Dastgir, which is a title usually attached to Abdul Qadir Gilani, a different Baghdad saint altogether.

## Dome, lattice and a table in the dark

The exterior photograph, probably from the early twentieth century, shows what looks like a modest compound: a mud-plastered wall with a lattice parapet, two low domes, a palm, a few men by a doorway, and the flat Mesopotamian horizon. It is not a monument. It is a house of respect, kept up by people who had no obligation to remember him.

## Found, repaired, lost

Sikh accounts say the shrine became known to Sikhs of the subcontinent during the First World War, when soldiers serving in Mesopotamia came across it and documented the plaque. It was repaired during and after the Second World War, and again later. The widely repeated final chapter is that the building was badly damaged in the 2003 war and the original plaque looted. I treat that as reported rather than proven; the accounts I have seen are community and encyclopaedic summaries and they vary. What I can say is that photographs like these may now be the best surviving record.

## From where I stand

I write this in a city that is a crossroads, as Baghdad was, and as Mecca was for the sea traders who came before oil. The Gulf has always been a place where people from elsewhere arrive with a trade, a language and a faith, and are judged by how they conduct themselves. There is a gurdwara a few minutes' drive from where many of us work, and on a day like this its langar feeds people of every background without asking a question.

The three instructions attached to Nanak's name, to remember the Name, to earn by honest work, and to share what you have, are not mystical. They read to me like an operating system for anyone building something: a discipline, an ethic of earning, and a duty to distribute. I try, imperfectly, to run my own ventures against that standard.

What stays with me about Baghdad is the vocabulary. The people there did not call him a founder, a prophet or a rival. They called him faqir, and they built him a room. If I had to choose one measure of a life, I would choose that: that strangers, centuries later and a thousand miles away, are still tending the place where you sat down.
