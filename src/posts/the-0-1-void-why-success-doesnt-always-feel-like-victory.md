We spend our lives building a blueprint. We map out the career, the city, the milestones, and the material acquisitions, and we're told that once we reach the "golden gate" of our own ambition, the view will be worth the climb. Lately I've been sitting with a question that seems to haunt everyone I know who has actually checked every box: why does it still feel like something is missing?

According to the plan, I should be happy. On paper, everything is exactly as it was supposed to be. I've achieved the things I set out to achieve, and there's still a weight to it—call it a 0.1% void—that refuses to fill in. It's the slow realization that the "end" we spent years running toward was never really an end. It was just the starting line of a new, more exhausting race, one we hadn't agreed to enter.

Ghalib understood this centuries before any of us had the language for burnout:

> *Hazaaron khwahishein aisi ki, har khwahish pe dum nikle.Desires are aplenty, and each one worth dying for.*

We fulfil so many of them and still wake up hungry for the next. That's the marathon of the modern world in one line—the conviction that the next promotion, the next car, the next upgrade will finally be the one that grants us peace, even though the last dozen didn't.

The trouble was never the race itself. It's the pace and the direction we've stopped questioning. When we run blind, we lose something like situational awareness for our own lives. We stop valuing what we already have because we're too busy coveting what someone else appears to. We move so fast that we miss the very life we were supposedly trying to improve. There's a line often credited to Albert Schweitzer—though its actual origins are murkier than the attribution suggests—that success is not the key to happiness; happiness is the key to success. Whoever said it first, we've spent years running the equation in reverse, and we're paying for it in exactly the currency we can least afford: our own internal peace.

None of this means the running has to stop. Life will always be some version of a race, whether you're climbing a ladder someone else built or constructing your own from nothing—the pressure doesn't disappear either way. What changes is whether you run with intention or just run. That starts with auditing your own desires honestly enough to tell the difference between what you actually want and what you were simply conditioned to want. It means defining your own "enough", because if the goalpost keeps moving on its own, you will never once get to feel like you've arrived, no matter how far you've come. And it means, more than anything else I've learned from any of this, checking the clarity of the faces around you. If you're running so fast that the people you love have started to blur at the edges of your vision, you are running too fast. No trophy is worth losing the connection to the people you are supposedly running for.

We so often lose what we have in the pursuit of what we want. The struggle is real. But the fix isn't complicated so much as it's difficult: a shift in where we've been looking for the finish line. Let's stop waiting for the perfect, arriving moment of happiness and start finding it in the stride itself. The race won't end until we do. 

The goal was never to cross more lines. It was to make sure that when we finally look back, what we see isn't a blur of milestones but a gallery of faces and moments that actually mattered.
