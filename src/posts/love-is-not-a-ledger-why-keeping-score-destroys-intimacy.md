There was a time in my life when I kept an invisible ledger for every relationship I cared about. I did not know I was doing it. It felt like simply being aware, paying attention, and keeping things fair. But fairness and love are not the same thing, and it took me years to understand why the relationships that mattered most to me kept feeling heavier instead of lighter.

The sentence that changed how I see everything is this: **"If love does not know how to give and take without restrictions, it is not love, but a transaction that never fails to lay stress on a plus and a minus."**

I want to unpack that sentence with you, because I believe it holds the key to why so many of us feel exhausted by the very people we love the most.

**The Invisible Balance Sheet**

When we view relationships through a ledger, every word, gesture, and effort gets recorded on an invisible balance sheet. We catch ourselves measuring who texted first, who compromised last, and who owes whom a favour.

I remember the exact moment I realized I was doing this. A close friend forgot to check in on me after a hard week. My first thought was not "I hope she is okay" or "I miss her." My first thought was, "After everything I did for her last month, she could not send one message?" That thought was not love speaking. That was a bookkeeper speaking. That was a plus and a minus talking.

The scary part is how natural it feels. We are raised in a world of exchanges. We earn wages for work. We pay for goods. We expect returns on investments. So when we enter love, we drag that same mental machinery with us, and we wonder why intimacy starts to feel like a negotiation.

*True love cannot survive in an environment ruled by bookkeeping.*

**The Danger of Transactional Love**

The danger of transactional love lies in its obsession with symmetry. A transaction demands immediate or calculated returns. When you buy something at a store, the exchange is clear: value for value. There is nothing wrong with that in commerce. But when this dynamic sneaks into personal relationships, affection becomes conditional, and care turns into leverage.

You find yourself thinking, **"I did this for you, so you must do that for me."**

I have said versions of this sentence in my own head more times than I want to admit. I have given gifts and then quietly waited for acknowledgement. I have planned dates and then silently tallied whether my partner planned the next one. I have helped friends move apartments and then felt a sting when they did not help me with something months later.

The moment love requires a guaranteed return on investment, it stops being love and becomes a contract.

And here is the cruellest part: the other person can feel it. They may not articulate it, but they sense that your kindness has a hidden invoice attached. That awareness poisons the air between you. A hug starts to feel like a payment. A favour starts to feel like a trap. Even a genuine compliment lands with a question mark behind it: *What does he want from me?*

**Hyper-Vigilance and the Death of Safety**

A contract creates an atmosphere of perpetual hyper-vigilance. You are constantly assessing whether you are in the "plus" or the "minus". If you feel you have given more, resentment builds. If you feel you owe more, guilt settles in.

I lived in that state for a long time without knowing there was another way. Every interaction carried an undercurrent of calculation. I would replay conversations in my head, trying to figure out who had been more generous, more patient, and more understanding. I kept score so obsessively that I could not simply enjoy the person in front of me.

This balance-sheet dynamic transforms intimate moments into negotiations and turns shared vulnerabilities into debts. Emotional safety vanishes because every act of kindness carries an unwritten cost.

When you cannot receive a gift without immediately wondering how you will repay it, you are not free. When you cannot offer help without quietly hoping for recognition, you are not free. And love, real love, requires freedom.

**What True Love Actually Looks Like**

True love, by contrast, operates on abundance rather than deficit. It gives freely without calculating the cost and receives gracefully without feeling burdened by debt.

I want to be careful here, because this is where people often misunderstand. Saying love gives freely does not mean sacrificing boundaries or staying in one-sided, abusive dynamics. It does not mean becoming a doormat. It does not mean tolerating neglect or mistreatment in the name of being "unconditional".

Rather, it means that the act of giving is itself the reward, not a deposit made toward a future withdrawal. You offer support, grace, and presence because you care, not because you are building credit for a rainy day.

There is a profound difference between giving because you want to and giving because you are afraid of what will happen if you do not. One is love. The other is self-protection dressed up as generosity.

When I finally started to loosen my grip on the ledger, I noticed something surprising. I became more generous, not less. I became more patient, not more resentful. Because I was no longer giving in order to get something back. I was giving because giving felt like the most natural expression of how I felt.

**Four Shifts That Changed My Relationships**

Moving away from the plus-and-minus mindset requires a conscious shift in perspective. These are the four shifts that helped me most.

**1. Give Without an Agenda**

Offer kindness, help, or affection purely to enrich the other person's life, without secretly expecting a specific reaction or payback.

This was the hardest one for me. I had to practice noticing when my giving came with strings. I had to ask myself, **Would I still do this if they never thanked me, never noticed, and never reciprocated?** If the answer was no, I had to be honest about why I was doing it.

Giving without an agenda does not mean you never feel disappointed. It means you do not use your disappointment as a weapon. It means you can say, "I gave because I wanted to, and I release the outcome."

**2. Receive Without Guilt**

Accept care and support fully, without immediately feeling the urge to "balance the scale" or pay it back.

This one surprised me. I thought I was bad at giving. It turned out I was even worse at receiving. Every time someone did something kind for me, I felt a quiet panic. I needed to reciprocate immediately, or I felt like I owed them something.

But receiving without guilt is an act of trust. It says, "I believe you are giving to me freely, and I will not insult that gift by turning it into a debt." It also gives the other person the gift of being able to give. When you refuse to receive, you rob them of that joy.

**3. Communicate Needs Directly**

Replace unspoken expectations and scorekeeping with clear, honest conversations about what you need.

Most of my resentment in relationships came from expectations I had never voiced. I wanted my partner to know what I needed without me having to say it. I wanted my friends to intuit when I was struggling. I wanted people to read my mind, and then I punished them for failing at a task I had never assigned.

Communicating needs directly is not demanding. It is not needy. It is honest. It is saying, "This is what I am feeling, and this is what would help me." It gives the other person a chance to show up for you, rather than setting them up to fail.

**4. Focus on Mutual Growth**

Reframe relationships not as two people trading resources, but as two people building a shared life together.

This is the big picture. When you see your relationship as a partnership rather than a transaction, the question changes. You stop asking, "What am I getting out of this?" and start asking, "What are we building together?"

A shared life is not always symmetrical. Sometimes one person carries more. Sometimes the other does. That is not a failure of fairness. That is the natural rhythm of two imperfect people loving each other through changing seasons.

**What Happens When You Drop the Calculator**

When we drop the emotional calculator, we open the door to genuine intimacy. Love ceases to be a tense negotiation and becomes a safe harbour where both people can give and receive freely, confident that they are valued for who they are, not for what they bring to the balance sheet.

I still catch myself keeping score sometimes. Old habits die hard. But now I notice it sooner, and I can ask myself: **Do I want to be right, or do I want to be close?** Do I want to win the ledger, or do I want to love this person well?

The answer, almost every time, is love.

Because love is not a transaction. It never was. It is a choice, made again and again, to give without keeping count and to receive without keeping score. It is the decision to trust that the person in front of you is not your debtor or your creditor but your partner, your friend, or your home.

And when you finally stop stressing the plus and the minus, you discover something you could never have earned or repaid: the quiet, steady peace of being loved for who you are.

**A Closing Thought**

If you have been exhausted by the relationships in your life, I want to ask you gently: Are you keeping a ledger? Are you measuring? Are you waiting for a return that may never come?

You do not have to live that way. You can put down the pen. You can stop balancing the scale. You can give because you want to, and receive because you are worthy, and trust that love does not need to be fair to be real.

Love is not a contract. It is not a transaction. It is not a plus and a minus.

It is a gift. And gifts, by their very nature, are not meant to be repaid.

They are meant to be received, cherished, and passed on.

That is the kind of love I want to live in. That is the kind of love I am still learning to give.

I hope this finds you somewhere on the same road, putting down your calculator, and opening your hands.
