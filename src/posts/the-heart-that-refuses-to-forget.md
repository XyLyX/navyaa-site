There are certain lines of poetry that do not merely describe a feeling—they become the feeling itself. Jaun Elia once wrote:

> *Sau baar kaha dil se, chal bhool bhi ja usko,*
>
> *Har baar kaha dil ne, tum dil se nahi kehte.*

A hundred times I said from the heart, "Come on, forget them too." And every time the heart replied, "You're not saying it from the heart."

I have lived inside those lines for longer than I care to admit.

Forgetting is supposed to be a skill we eventually learn. Time, they say, is the great healer. Distance is the quiet teacher. New routines, new people, and new mornings are meant to dilute the old ache until it becomes background noise. I have tried all of it. I have told myself the rational things a hundred times, a thousand times. I have spoken to my own heart with the firmness of someone who wants to move forward. And every single time, the heart has answered with the same quiet, devastating honesty: you don't mean it. Not really.

There are days when the struggle feels almost physical. I wake up determined. I make coffee, open the laptop, answer messages, and move through the hours with the practiced discipline of someone who has decided that this chapter is closed. Then a song plays in a café. A particular shade of light falls across the floor at the same angle it once did. A sentence in a book echoes something once said. And suddenly the carefully constructed wall collapses. The heart does not shout. It simply leans in and whispers the truth. I already know: you have not forgotten. You have only been pretending.

I used to think this was the heart being difficult—sentimental, unreasonable, refusing to cooperate with a mind that had clearly moved on. But lately I've started to wonder whether the heart is even the one doing the refusing. The heart negotiates. It bargains, it rationalizes, it tries out new arguments each morning like a lawyer building a case it doesn't fully believe. Underneath the heart's daily arguing, though, there's something quieter and far less talkative—something that was never asked whether it wanted to forget, because it doesn't operate on the terms of wanting at all. Call it the soul if the word still means anything to you. The soul does not negotiate. It only recognizes. And once it has recognized something as true, no amount of daily persuasion from the mind reaches down far enough to unmake it.

What hurts more than the longing itself is the quiet, persistent "what if". On certain evenings, when the house is still and the mind has nowhere left to hide, the thought arrives uninvited: if life had taken a different turn. If the conversations had been longer. If the silences had been shorter. If the timing had been kinder. If I had been understood a little more clearly, held a little more carefully. Would I have been standing somewhere else today—lighter, quieter, more at peace? Would the version of me that still carries this weight have been allowed to rest?

These thoughts are dangerous because they feel so reasonable. They do not demand the past back. They only ask whether a different path might have led to a place where the heart did not have to argue with itself every morning. They suggest that the ache is not only about loss but also about the quiet conviction that something essential was missed—an understanding that never fully arrived, a belonging that remained just out of reach. And beneath even that suggestion sits a stranger, older question: whether the soul recognized something in that person, or in that chapter, that it has not found permission to release simply because the calendar moved forward. The soul, unlike the mind, keeps no calendar at all. It has no sense of how long is too long to still be standing at the same door.

And yet, something in me continues its stubborn work. It refuses the tidy narrative of "moving on". It does not care about the calendar or the advice of well-meaning friends. It remembers the texture of a voice, the shape of a particular silence, and the way certain days used to feel possible. It keeps the memory alive not out of cruelty, but out of a strange, unbroken loyalty to what once felt true—the kind of loyalty that has nothing to do with hope of return and everything to do with simply refusing to call something false that once, unmistakably, was not.

I do not know when, or if, the heart will finally agree with the mind. Some days I still believe it will. Other days I suspect this quiet argument may simply be part of who I am now—a person who tried, sincerely and repeatedly, to forget and discovered that some attachments do not dissolve just because we ask them to, because they were never lodged in the part of us that takes orders in the first place.

Jaun Elia's words remain the most honest description I have found of this condition. The mind can issue its orders a hundred times. The heart will keep answering, gently but firmly, that the words were never spoken from the deepest place. And beneath the heart, keeping its own separate silence, the soul simply waits—not for the forgetting to arrive, but for the day the ache is no longer mistaken for a wound and is finally recognized for what it may have been all along: a form of devotion that never asked to be resolved.

So I continue. I live the days. I build the new life. I practice the art of moving forward. And somewhere beneath all of it, something older than the heart keeps its quiet vigil—yearning, remembering, and refusing, still, to say the words it does not yet believe.
