I turned fifty-two this year. And if half a century of living—with all its bruises and beauty—has taught me anything, it's how to tell the difference between something real and something that just sounds like it.

We live in a strange time. We confuse access with intimacy. We carry little glowing rectangles in our pockets that buzz and flash and demand our attention like needy children. We mistake a quick text reply for being present. We mistake a "like" for affection. We mistake read receipts for accountability.

But if I'm honest with myself—if I strip away the comfortable lies I tell at dinner parties and scroll past on social media—I'm not more connected at all. I'm just relentlessly, exhaustingly available.

In my twenties and thirties, I was all in on the digital dream. I believed technology would shrink the world, bridge every divide, and bring us closer. What it actually did was build a hyper-efficient machine for harvesting my attention—while leaving my spirit completely unfed.

Availability is mechanical. It means your boss can reach you on a Sunday night. It means a distant acquaintance can drop an unsolicited opinion into your morning coffee. It means you're reachable by everyone, everywhere, all the time.

But connection? Connection is sacred. It requires vulnerability, stillness, and the terrifying courage to be seen—messy, unpolished, fully human. You can't download that. You can't automate it. And you certainly can't measure it in follower counts.

Life has a way of stripping away your illusions. Usually through loss. Heartbreak. The sudden, gut-punch realisation that time is running out.

When tragedy strikes—when a marriage ends, when a diagnosis arrives, when a parent passes—the digital crowd vanishes like smoke. You learn, in those dark nights, the worthlessness of our performative kindnesses. We post the right sympathies. We adopt the trendy outrage of the week. We offer "thoughts and prayers" via emoji while refusing to sit quietly with a friend who is grieving.

We surrendered depth for breadth. We built a world of pixels and profiles because reality is painful, unpredictable, and demanding. In our fake world, we control the narrative. We edit our responses. We hide our flaws. We block anyone who challenges our fragile self-image. And in doing so, we starved ourselves of the very thing that makes life endurable: genuine human warmth.

When you're always available, you belong to everyone except yourself. Your brain becomes fragmented, leaping from one shallow interaction to the next. You're a node in a network, reacting instead of living. I've felt this in my bones.

Real relationships require navigating friction, awkward silences, and uncomfortable truths—face-to-face. Screen-mediated lives let us bypass all of that, and our emotional muscles grow weak for it. At the first sign of real conflict, we retreat into isolation. And when every moment is shareable, you stop living experiences for yourself—you start living them for an audience, curating your joy rather than feeling it.

You cannot truly connect with others if you can't tolerate being alone with your own mind. Constant availability cures boredom, but it destroys solitude—the quiet space where self-knowledge and inner peace are forged.

I don't say any of this from a place of superiority. I say it from the quiet clarity that comes after years of chasing the wrong things—and paying the price for it.

If you want to escape the illusion, you have to start making deliberate, uncomfortable choices. Protect your boundaries ruthlessly—unavailability is not selfishness; it is the precondition for depth. Turn off the notifications. Let the phone ring. Stop answering the second it buzzes. Demand presence over proximity: a thirty-minute face-to-face conversation, with both phones in another room, is worth more than ten thousand text messages sent over six months. And kill the audience—do something beautiful, meaningful, or painful today without documenting it for anyone else. Keep it for your soul alone.

We don't need more followers, faster connections, or smarter devices. We need the courage to put down the glass screens, look each other in the eye, and admit how lonely we've allowed ourselves to become—in a world that never stops talking.

This is what fifty-two has taught me. I'm still learning. But at least now I know what I'm looking for.
