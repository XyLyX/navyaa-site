*On resistance, motivation, and the quiet engine of showing up*

For years, I believed I was waiting for something. I never said it aloud, but it shaped my mornings: the right book, the right playlist, a clean desk, a slow coffee, and then, surely, the spark would arrive and the hard thing would become easy.

It rarely did. And when it did, it never lasted past lunch.

## The task at the top of the list

Every day, there is one item on my list that carries weight. I can feel it before I read it. Some days it is a page I haven't started writing. Some days it is a phone call I have rehearsed in my head more often than I have dialled. Sometimes it is a stubborn bottleneck in one of the businesses I run, the kind of problem that sits quietly in the corner of every meeting and waits for someone to say its name.

I have learned to recognise the voice that comes with it. It is calm, reasonable, almost kind. *You're not in the right headspace today. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe this isn't even the right direction.*

For a long time I mistook that voice for wisdom. I thought my discomfort was information, a signal that I should stop and reconsider. Now I think it is something simpler. The discomfort is the price of entry.

## Resistance is a toll booth, not a stop sign

I have spent more than twenty-five years inside operations: contact centres, client programmes, systems, teams, and eventually ventures of my own. If that life taught me one thing, it is that the people who looked lucky from the outside were almost never lucky. They were simply the ones who kept doing the dull, unglamorous thing after everyone else had decided it was beneath them or beyond them.

What we call magic, the overnight success, the effortless skill, the sudden momentum is mostly a delayed payment. Someone made a deposit every day for years, in small coins nobody applauded. Then one morning the balance became visible, and the world called it a breakthrough.

The work I avoid is not blocking the door to what I want. It is standing right in front of it, holding the key, and waiting for me to stop pretending I haven't seen it.

## The fair-weather friend

I have nothing against motivation. I love it the way I love a clear sky in Dubai in winter: wonderful, generous, and not to be planned around.

When I start something new, motivation is everywhere. The idea is fresh, the possibilities are loud, and every hour feels productive. When the first good feedback arrives, I could work till dawn. On those days I mistake energy for character.

But the days that decide whether something becomes real are not those days. They are the grey ones. I am tired. Something else has gone wrong. The work has gone from exciting to merely long, and nothing on the screen shows that any of it is working. On those days motivation does not turn up, and it never apologises.

If my output depends on how I feel when I wake up, then my progress belongs to my mood. And moods, as anyone knows, are poor project managers.

## What consistency really costs

When motivation leaves, consistency is the only engine still running. It is also, honestly, the least attractive thing I know.

There is no applause for another quiet hour when nothing dramatic is happening. There is no small reward for maintaining a standard that nobody is checking. On the hardest days, the only visible result is that I did not drop the ball. That is not a story anyone will tell at dinner.

And yet this is where the leverage lives. Small, repeatable effort compounds silently, long after enthusiasm has packed up and gone home. The strange gift of consistency is not that the work becomes easier. It does not. The gift is that I stop negotiating. The debate in my head, the daily courtroom drama of *should I or shouldn't I*, simply ends. The decision has already been made, by a version of me who was thinking clearly.

I have come to treat that as a form of self-respect. Not discipline in the harsh sense, but a kind of promise kept to the person I will be next month.

## How I try to bridge the gap

I am not writing this from the far side of the problem. I still avoid things. I still reorganise my workspace when I should be writing. What has changed is how I handle the moment of resistance. Three small habits help:

**I name the heaviest task.** Not the most urgent, the heaviest. I ask which single thing, if finished, would make everything else lighter. It is almost always the one I have been quietly moving to tomorrow.

**I shrink the commitment until it is almost embarrassing.** Ten minutes. One paragraph. One phone call. I do not promise myself the mountain, only the first few steps. Remarkably often, the mountain turns out to be smaller once I am standing on it, and when it isn't, I have still moved.

**I measure the streak, not just the outcome.** On low days, success is simply not breaking the chain. Showing up at sixty percent beats staying away at zero, and it keeps the door open for the day I show up at a hundred.

## What I know now

I used to think the question was how to feel ready. I now think that is the wrong question, and that it has cost me more time than any difficult task ever did.

The better question is smaller and more honest: *what am I avoiding today, and can I give it ten minutes?*

The magic I went looking for, in books and podcasts and tidy desks, was never hiding anywhere clever. It was inside the thing I was walking around. So I am learning to walk straight into it, without applause, without waiting to feel ready, and let consistency do the quiet work of building something I can eventually call my own.

Start with the thing you have been avoiding. Give it ten minutes. The rest will follow, slowly, and then all at once.
