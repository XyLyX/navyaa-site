*We've mistaken "breathing" for "living" for far too long.*

## Understanding the Great Masquerade

We're taught, from a young age, to think of death as a finale — loud, dramatic, a sudden stop marked by ceremony and a collective outpouring of grief. We build rituals for it because we can see it: the moment arrives, and the world responds accordingly. But there's another kind of death that never makes it into an obituary, never gets a ceremony, and yet quietly claims people every day. It's the internal erosion that happens while everyone around you assumes you're doing just fine.

A short poem has been sitting with me for weeks because of how precisely it names this:

> *I died quietly,painfully,a death no one grievedbecauseI kept breathing,and breathing looks a lot like livingif you're not paying attention.*

What the poem is naming, I think, is a kind of masquerade — not a deliberate deception, but a survival mechanism so effective it fools even the person performing it. If you're showing up to work, replying to messages, and keeping pace with the group chat, the world reasonably assumes you're alive in every sense that counts. But biological function and human vitality are not the same thing, and the gap between them can widen for years without anyone, including you, noticing it happen. Breathing, in this sense, is just the engine idling — technically running, going nowhere. True living asks for more: engagement, hope, and the sense of being tethered to something or someone beyond the next task on the list. When those things quietly disappear, a person can undergo a total internal implosion while still, outwardly, performing every function of a normal life. Because there's no visible wreckage, there's nothing that reads, from the outside, as needing to be grieved.

## The Cost of Ignoring Internal Struggles

The danger of the great masquerade isn't only that it deceives other people — it's that it deceives the person wearing it too. When you can still perform every external function of a life that's working, it becomes remarkably easy to postpone the question of whether it's actually working. There's no single moment that forces the issue. No crisis arrives to demand your attention, because from the outside, and often from the inside as well, everything appears to be under control.

But ignored long enough, this kind of internal erosion doesn't stay contained. It leaks into the quality of your relationships, even the ones that look intact — conversations become transactional, presence becomes performance, and the people closest to you start relating to a slightly hollowed-out version of who you used to be, without being able to name exactly what changed. It leaks into your relationship with your own future, flattening ambition into obligation and curiosity into routine. And perhaps most insidiously, it teaches you that this is simply what adulthood, or responsibility, or maturity feels like — a low hum of disengagement mistaken for stability. The cost of ignoring it isn't usually a dramatic collapse. It's a slow subtraction, so gradual that by the time you notice what's missing, you've nearly forgotten what having it felt like.

## Why We Fail to Notice Each Other

The most piercing line in that poem, for me, is the small qualifier at the end: *if you're not paying attention.* It quietly redirects some of the responsibility outward. The tragedy isn't only the internal death itself — it's our collective failure to notice it happening in the people directly in front of us.

Part of this is simply how we've been trained to look for crisis. We watch for the loud signs — the outburst, the visible tears, the sudden shutdown — because those are legible, urgent, and impossible to miss. In scanning for those signals, we miss the far more common and far quieter ones: the friend whose laugh has gone slightly hollow, the colleague who answers every question correctly but seems to be relaying information from somewhere further away than usual, and the family member who has become reliably, suspiciously fine. Part of it, too, is the sheer pace of modern life, which rewards surface-level check-ins — the "how are you", answered with "good, you?" — over anything that requires slowing down long enough to actually look. We see people constantly. We rarely have the structure, or the permission, to actually perceive them.

## The Importance of Seeing Beyond the Surface

There's a meaningful difference between being around someone and actually seeing them, and most of our daily interactions live entirely in the first category. Seeing beyond the surface means treating "I'm fine" as a sentence that might be true, might be a placeholder, or might be a door quietly left ajar for someone willing to knock rather than walk past. It means noticing not just what someone says, but the shape of what they've stopped saying — the joke they used to make that hasn't come up in months, the plans they used to be excited about that now get mentioned flatly, if at all.

This kind of seeing isn't about diagnosing people or assuming crisis where none exists — that would be its own kind of surface-level reading, just inverted. It's about staying curious rather than settling for the first plausible answer, and being willing to sit in the slightly uncomfortable space of not immediately knowing whether someone is actually okay. Most people are, in fact, fine most of the time. But the ones who aren't are rarely going to announce it, and the world has made it easier than it should be to take a well-rehearsed "fine" at face value.

## How to Cultivate Empathy

None of this happens automatically, and it isn't a fixed trait some people have and others don't — it's closer to a skill, built through small, repeatable habits.

It starts with asking better questions than the reflexive "how are you," which has become so ritualized it barely functions as a question anymore. Something as simple as "how are you, actually" or "what's been the hardest part of your week" invites a different kind of answer, and signals that you're prepared to receive one.

It continues with noticing change over time rather than only reacting to a single moment — the friend who used to text constantly and has gone quiet, the colleague whose energy in meetings has flattened over a few months. These shifts are usually more diagnostic than any single conversation, but they require paying attention across time, not just in the moment.

It also means resisting the urge to fix things immediately. Often what people need first isn't a solution, but the experience of being accurately seen without being rushed toward resolution — sitting with someone's "I don't know" instead of filling the silence with advice.

And it means making yourself into the kind of person others feel safe being honest with, which usually has less to do with saying the right thing and more to do with how you've responded the last several times someone told you something true and difficult. Empathy, cultivated this way, isn't a feeling you either have or don't. It's a practice, built one honest conversation at a time.

## A Call to Action: Look Deeper

So here is the ask, stated plainly: look closer at the people around you who seem to be managing just fine, precisely because they seem to be managing just fine. Look at the friend who always has it together, the colleague who never complains, and the family member who has perfected the art of being low-maintenance. Not out of suspicion, but out of the recognition that ease can be a performance, and that some of the people who most need someone to look closer are the ones who have gotten remarkably good at making sure no one has to.

You don't need training or credentials to do this. You need only the willingness to ask a real question and actually wait for the real answer and the patience to keep showing up even when the first few answers are still "I'm fine." Sometimes the most important thing you can do for another person is simply notice the quiet death happening in front of you before it finishes happening — and be the reason someone finds their way back before the distance becomes permanent.

## Finding Our Way Back to True Living

True living, when I try to actually define it rather than just gesture at its absence, seems to come down to three things: engagement with what you're doing rather than mere completion of it, hope that stretches further than the next obligation, and connection that doesn't require translation to be understood. None of these are dramatic. None of them require a complete life overhaul. They tend to return the way they left — quietly, in small increments, one honest conversation or one moment of real presence at a time.

If any part of this essay has landed uncomfortably close to home — if you recognized your own breathing more than your own living somewhere in these lines — that recognition is itself the beginning of the way back. You don't have to solve it today. You only have to stop mistaking the idling engine for forward motion and let someone close enough to notice the difference. And if you're the one doing the noticing, know that your attention, however small it feels, might be the exact thing standing between someone and the quiet, ungrieved ending this essay describes. Breathing looks a lot like living, right up until someone finally pays attention. Be the one who does.
