Every year, on the thirteenth lunar day of the dark fortnight, the first lamps of Diwali are lit. The day is Dhanteras, or Dhantrayodashi. *Dhan* means wealth and *Teras* means the thirteenth. Most of us hear the name and think of jewellers' windows and shopping lists. I want to suggest that the stories behind the day are asking for something else.

## The healer comes first

According to the Puranas, the devas and asuras churned the cosmic ocean to draw out its treasures. On this day Lord Dhanvantari rose from the water. He is the physician of the gods and an avatar of Vishnu, and he carried a pot of the nectar of immortality along with the knowledge of Ayurveda. Lakshmi and Kubera, the givers and keepers of wealth, belong to the same season.

Look at the order of the story. The first thing the ocean gave up on this day was a healer holding medicine, before any coin or jewel. We say "health is wealth" so often that it has gone dull. The legend is older than the saying, and it means it.

## The wife who would not let him sleep

The second story is the one I love most. A king's son, just sixteen, was predicted to die of a snakebite on the fourth night of his marriage. His new wife refused to let him sleep. She piled her gold and silver at the doorway, lit lamps through the whole house, and sat up singing and telling stories.

When Yamraj, the Lord of Death, came in the form of a serpent, the glitter and light blinded him. He settled on the heap of treasure and listened until the fatal hour had passed, and then he slipped away. That is why a Yamadeep is lit at night on Dhanteras, and why gold and silver are bought today.

She did not fight death. She stayed awake, made the room bright and kept her husband company. I read it as a story about attention. Most of what protects the people we love is ordinary and patient: being present, keeping the light on, talking through the dark hours.

## The farmer and the goddess

The third legend has Lakshmi boasting that she controls all the wealth in the world. Vishnu replies that real wealth lies in hard work and in feeding others. She goes down to earth and blesses the fields of a poor, hardworking farmer for twelve years. Prosperity, here, follows effort and generosity, and the cleaning of homes and lighting of lamps is how we make room for it.

## What it asks of us now

I do not think these stories are asking us to give up gold. Buying a coin, a piece of silver or a brass vessel today has grown into something broader: a day to plan, to invest, or to buy one lasting thing with care. That is a good habit. But I notice how rarely the other half of the day gets the same attention.

We live in a time of burnout, poor sleep and constant [noise. Dhanvantari's pot of medicine](/how-to-find-peace-when-life-feels-overwhelming/) is a fair reminder to book the check-up we have been postponing, to walk, to eat properly and to rest. The wife's lamps are a reminder to clear our spaces and our heads. The deep cleaning before Diwali is also a chance to [put down mental clutter, the worries we carry](/finding-clarity-in-a-noisy-world-my-path-to-intentional-living/) from room to room. And the farmer's story reminds us that what we share tends to grow.

## A small practice for the day

* Clean or declutter one corner of your home.
* Light a lamp at the entrance as evening falls.
* Make one considered purchase, if you wish, and make it deliberately.
* Do one thing for your health that you have been putting off.
* Feed, call or thank someone who would not expect it.

## A wish

As we light the first diyas of the season, I wish you a bright and peaceful festive week. When we ask for *dhan* today, may we look beyond gold and silver to the greater wealth: our health, our peace of mind, and the laughter of the people we love. Protect your inner light, and make the world a little brighter for someone else.

Happy Dhanteras. Keep shining. 🪔
