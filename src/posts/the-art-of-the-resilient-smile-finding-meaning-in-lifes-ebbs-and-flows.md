In a world that constantly demands more—more success, more possessions, more proof that we are doing life correctly—we tend to forget the simplest human response to existence: the smile. A piece of poetry I keep coming back to, *Muskurayo* ("Smile"), challenges the reflex we've all been trained into, the one where hardship is met with analysis, complaint, and comparison. It suggests something quieter and stranger: that a smile isn't only the result of happiness. It can be the tool itself—a way through, not just a reaction to having arrived.

> **Muskurayo, muskurayo agar aaj kahin se haar gaye ho, us jeet ki zarurat tumse zyada kisi ko thi shayad.**
>
> *Smile, smile if you have lost today; perhaps someone else needed that victory more than you.*
>
> **Muskurayo, agar kuch kho gaya hai, jis ke naseeb ka tha usko mil gaya hai shayad.**
>
> *Smile if you have lost something; perhaps it was destined for the person who found it.*
>
> **Muskurayo, agar dil toot gaya hai, kisi ka jodne ke liye kisi ka todna padta hoga shayad.**
>
> *Smile if your heart is broken; perhaps one heart must break to mend another.*
>
> **Aur phir bhi reh jaye agar dil mein dard kahin, to baant kar muskurayo.**
>
> *And if there is still pain left in your heart, share it and smile.*
>
> **Aur hai agar dil mein khushi zyada, to same process dohrayo.**
>
> *And if there is an abundance of joy in your heart, repeat the same process.*
>
> **Muskurayo, agar sar par hai chat, badan par hai kapda, aur hai thali mein khana, aur hai agar zarurat se zyada, to baant kar ghar jana.**
>
> *Smile if you have a roof over your head, clothes on your body, and food on your plate; and if you have more than you need, share it before you go home.*
>
> **Muskurayo, jab baar-baar ye soch kar hatash ho jate ho ki is se accha ye ho jata, is se accha wo ho jata, tab ye soch kar muskurayo ki is se bura ho jata to kya ho jata.**
>
> *Smile when you feel despondent thinking "this or that could have been better"; instead, smile thinking about how much worse it could have been.*
>
> **Muskurayo, jab pooche koi ki zindagi jeene ka hai kya tareeqa, muskurayo ye keh kar ki hum ne zindagi se muskurana hi seekha.**
>
> *Smile when someone asks what is the best way to live life; smile and say that we have learned nothing from life but how to smile.*

The poem opens by reframing loss and defeat entirely. If you lose, perhaps someone else needs that win more. If something of yours goes missing, perhaps it was simply someone else's destiny. We tend to treat life as a zero-sum game, where our loss is automatically a tragedy—but the poet is proposing something closer to a universal connection running underneath all our separate stories. Once your loss can be someone else's quiet blessing, the ego drops out of the equation. Envy turns, almost without your noticing, into a kind of silent charity.

That same logic carries into what happens to the heart. If it breaks, maybe that breaking was the cost of mending another. And more practically: if there is pain in you, share it, so it lessens; if there is joy in you, share it, so it multiplies. This is emotional interdependence stated as plainly as it can be stated—the acknowledgement that our hearts were never meant to function in isolation. The instruction to "repeat the same process" with joy as with grief is the whole philosophy in miniature: vulnerability isn't a flaw to manage privately, it's a form of strength, and sharing your light is not so different from sharing your burden. Both are how a person finds their way back into community.

Then the poem grounds itself in something almost embarrassingly basic: a roof, clothing, and food on a plate. And if there is more than that, share it before you go home. This is a reality check aimed squarely at how most of our daily suffering is actually high-altitude anxiety, worry about status and comparison and what could have been, floating far above the actual floor of survival. The poem's quiet claim is that "enough" is already a feast and that anything beyond enough was never meant to be hoarded. It was meant to move.

The most philosophically dense verse comes near the end, in the instruction to stop grieving the better version of events that didn't happen and instead consider how much worse things could have gone. This is close to what the Stoics called *premeditatio malorum*—the premeditation of evils—not as pessimism, but as a way of recalibrating gratitude by remembering how much lower the floor of human suffering actually runs than where we currently stand. It isn't about minimizing pain. It's about noticing, honestly, the mercy embedded in the present moment.

None of this asks you to escape pain or manufacture a permanent state of bliss—that was never the promise. What it asks for is smaller and harder: the grace to keep moving through the highs and the lows without losing your shape. The next time a small thing goes wrong, there's a version of relief available in simply telling yourself someone else needed it more today. The next time you're tempted to share only your highlights, there's a version of connection available in showing someone your actual weather instead. And the next time your mind drifts toward what you don't have, there's a version of gratitude available in looking, without pity, at the roof that already holds you.

As the poem closes, so does this: the best way to live is simply to tell the world that you have learned nothing from life but how to smile.
