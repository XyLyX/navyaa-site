*I Deleted 530 Facebook Friends. Nobody Noticed.*

We built a global panopticon of applause, called it “social media,” and convinced ourselves that a pile of strangers, forgotten schoolmates and remote acquaintances amounts to a community. It doesn’t. It’s a vanity mirror that trades human friction for algorithmic dopamine.

For years I carried roughly 700 Facebook “friends,” collected over decades from every corner of life: business associates, passing acquaintances, people whose names I barely recalled, all filed neatly behind profile pictures. It felt like wealth. I never audited it.

Then I started paying attention. Out of 700 connections, barely five names appeared consistently in my notifications. Five. The other 695 might as well have been cardboard cutouts in an empty stadium.

So I ran an experiment and started deleting them in batches. I expected something: a message, a confused question, a flicker of offence. Did anyone ask why? Not a whisper. No outrage, no goodbye. Today I’m down to 170, and I suspect I’ll end up under 20. The rest were never friends. They were digital real estate on someone else’s server.

**The Number Was Never Going to Work**

My five wasn’t a coincidence. The anthropologist Robin Dunbar has argued for decades that human social life has layers: roughly 5 people in your innermost circle, about 15 good friends, about 50 close-ish friends, and around 150 meaningful contacts. Past that, you’re not maintaining relationships. You’re maintaining a list.

Facebook ignored that ceiling because the ceiling doesn’t scale as a business. A platform needs you to feel that more is better. So it handed us a number with no upper limit and let our egos supply the rest.

**The Architecture of the Illusion**

The platforms didn’t stumble into this wasteland. They monetized it, turning our need to belong into a metric.

* **The follower-count narcotic:**

   A big number whispers that you’re popular and relevant. It cushions fragile egos, and every new request is a small hit of validation.
* **The comfort of proximity:**

   Hundreds of ghosts let you feel connected without the work of a relationship. Accepting a request from someone you haven’t spoken to since 2008 takes zero vulnerability and gives you zero in return.
* **The algorithm as gatekeeper:**

   Even if you wanted to keep up with 700 people, the platform won’t let you. It filters, ranks and buries. You see the same predictable loop, and your updates sink into an abyss. The network is held together by code, not by human choice.
* **The audience problem:**

   Posting to 700 people teaches you to write for nobody in particular. You sand down your opinions and perform a safe, flattering version of yourself, because anyone might be watching. The result is a life edited for strangers and shared with no one.

**The Brutal Reality Check**

Cull the list and the veil drops. Nearly all of the network turns out to be silent spectators, data-harvesters, and people too indifferent to unfollow you. You also learn something about yourself: you were never close to most of them either. The relationship was mutual neglect, dressed up as connection.

We’ve let quantity murder depth. We swap two or three real confidants, the people who show up when life fractures, for a red notification badge tapped by a stranger. It’s processed sugar for a body starving for nutrition: full of empty calories and still hollow.

There’s a quieter cost as well. A thousand weak ties absorb attention that a few strong ones need. Every hour spent scrolling through the lives of people you barely know is an hour not spent calling the person who would drop everything for you. Attention is finite. Whatever you spend on the crowd, you take from the few.

**Weak Ties Aren’t Worthless. They Just Aren’t Friends**

In fairness, acquaintances have real value. Sociologists have long noted that weak ties bring us jobs, introductions and new ideas that our close circle can’t. The mistake isn’t having acquaintances. It’s mislabeling them.

A professional network is a tool. A friendship is a bond. When you call both “friends,” you start expecting loyalty from a directory and offering intimacy to a contact list. Keep your acquaintances where they belong, in a professional network or a quiet contact book, and stop counting them as proof that you are loved.

**A Simple Audit**

If you want to try this yourself, ask four questions of every name on your list:

1. Have we had a real conversation in the last two years?
2. Would I call this person if something went wrong?
3. Would they call me?
4. Would I notice if they vanished tomorrow?

If the answer to all four is no, you’re not keeping a friend. You’re keeping a statistic.

**Down to the Bone**

There is real freedom in watching the number fall. You stop performing for an audience that doesn’t exist and curating your life for people who wouldn’t recognize you on the street. You accept that most people are too absorbed in their own lives to care about yours, and that stops being depressing and becomes liberating. The people who remain are there because they chose to be, which makes their presence worth something.

Social media’s promise was that you’d never be alone. Its quiet effect was to make it harder to tell who was actually there. A smaller list brings back the signal. You can see who answers, who shows up, and who was only ever a name.

True connection can’t be scaled, automated or measured in digits. It takes skin in the game, shared silence and real-world friction. It costs time, honesty and a willingness to be seen.

Let the follower counts fall if they have to. Keep the five. Let the rest burn.
