There's a strange moment that arrives without warning. You wake up one day and realize you're living a life you never consciously chose. Not a bad life. Not even an unsuccessful one. Just unfamiliar. And the most unsettling part isn't where you are — it's who you've become on the way here.

A poem was found recently; in that particular way, certain lines find you exactly when you're ready to hear them:

> Shayad main bhool gaya hoon ki mujhe kya banna tha.
> *Perhaps I have forgotten what I once wanted to become.*
>
> Main bhool gaya hoon ki maine shuruaat kahan se kari thi.
> *I have forgotten where I even started from.*
>
> Kyonki samay hi kahan hai ye ab sochne ka.
> *Because there is simply no time left to think about these things anymore.*
>
> Kabhi-kabhi lagta hai main wo ban gaya hoon jo main banna nahi chahta tha.
> *Sometimes it feels like I have become the very person I never wanted to be.*
>
> Aur ab kabhi-kabhi lagta hai ki jo mujhe banna tha, wo mujhse bahut zyada door khada hai.
> *And now, sometimes it feels like the person I was meant to be is standing very, very far away from me.*

It captures the feeling more precisely than I've ever managed to on my own. What it names is identity drift — the slow, almost invisible distance that grows between who you once wanted to be and who you are now. No dramatic fall. No single wrong decision. Just time doing what it does best: moving forward while you're busy surviving.

At some point, the gap widened. The person I admired — the version of me who felt alive, curious, and certain — now feels like someone I once knew and lost touch with. And the irony is what actually hurts: I didn't become someone I dislike on purpose. I just became someone I never intended to be. That realization forces a question I'd spent years avoiding — when, exactly, did I stop choosing and start drifting?

In the beginning there was clarity. Not clarity of strategy, but clarity of why. The plans were simple and unburdened by practicality or fear. I never asked whether something was possible or profitable — I only knew it mattered. That version of me wasn't wiser. It was honest. It trusted instinct over approval, energy over security, and purpose over validation. That was the seed.

The drift didn't arrive as rebellion. It arrived disguised as responsibility. Life happened in small, reasonable steps — bills that needed paying, expectations that had to be met, and stability that started sounding smarter than passion. Slowly, the noise of survival drowned out the quiet voice of intention. I told myself things were temporary. I'll do this for now. I'll get back to myself later. But later never announced itself. There was no time to think, no space to reflect. I wasn't steering anymore — I was only responding. And when you live in reaction mode long enough, direction simply disappears.

Here's the uncomfortable truth the poem hints at, one I didn't want to look at directly for a long time: you can succeed in a life that doesn't belong to you. And when that happens, achievement feels hollow. Milestones feel strangely quiet. You reach places you once dreamed of, only to discover the dream belonged to someone you no longer fully recognize. That's when the quiet crisis appears — not because you failed, but because you succeeded at the wrong thing. The original you doesn't vanish in a moment like that. It just moves farther away, and one day you catch yourself fearing it might become a stranger.

The way back isn't to burn everything down or rewind the clock. Growth was never about erasing who you've become — it's about integrating who you were. It starts with returning to your why: setting aside real, uninterrupted time and asking yourself, honestly, what made you feel alive before you learned to ask what would impress anyone else. That answer hasn't left. It's still there, quiet and patient, waiting to be asked again. It continues with choosing one small act of truth rather than attempting a full overhaul — fifteen minutes, one habit, one promise you don't negotiate away, because identity is rarely reclaimed through grand gestures. It's returned through consistency.

It also asks for space to think, which is harder to protect than it sounds. Silence isn't laziness — it's a kind of leadership over your own life. Even ten minutes of real stillness can interrupt autopilot living and remind you that a choice still exists where you'd stopped looking for one. And it asks for honesty about the mask itself: naming, plainly, the traits in yourself you've grown uncomfortable with. Awareness alone doesn't fix anything, but it does something almost as important — once a pattern is named, it loses some of its grip simply by being seen.

Last, and hardest, it asks for forgiveness of the drift itself. The person you are today survived things your younger self never had to imagine. The drift wasn't weakness. It was adaptation — a reasonable response to an unreasonable amount of noise. That strength deserves honouring before it gets redirected, not instead of being redirected.

This isn't a story of failure. It's a story of forgetting and of slowly remembering. The original you isn't gone. It's just been quiet while you were busy becoming capable — and maybe now, finally, capable enough to return. Not as who you were, but as who you were always meant to become. The journey back doesn't require starting over. It only asks that you start listening again.
