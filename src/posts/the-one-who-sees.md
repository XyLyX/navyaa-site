When I first heard the line, it stopped me cold: kill my demons and my angels might die too. I was sitting in my room under the particular weight of self-improvement culture—a thousand books telling me to fix myself, eliminate my weaknesses, and become the best version of me. Every podcast, every article, every well-meaning friend seemed to be saying some version of the same thing: get rid of the parts of you that don't work, cut out the darkness, erase the flaws, and become complete. That single sentence made me pause in a way I hadn't paused in years.

There's a voice inside me I've come to think of as The One Who Sees. She isn't the loudest voice—she doesn't scream like anxiety does, or rush like ambition does, or judge like the inner critic does. She's quieter than all of them, sitting somewhere in the back of my mind, waiting for the moments when I'm still enough to actually hear her. And what she said, the night this line found me, cracked something open: if you kill the parts of you that scare you, you might also kill the parts of you that save you.

For years I thought of my demons as enemies. The anxiety that tightens my chest before social situations. The overthinking that keeps me awake at two in the morning replaying conversations I can't undo. The sensitivity that makes me feel everything a size too large. The need for approval that makes me smaller than I actually am. I wanted them gone, desperately—tried to meditate them away, positive-think them into silence, outrun them with achievements and busyness, and hide them even from myself. And The One Who Sees asked, gently, the question that undid all of it: what if those demons aren't invaders? What if they're guardians wearing scary masks?

Because the anxiety I hated so much is also the part of me that prepares carefully, that notices what others miss, and that cares enough not to hurt people—the same energy that makes me a thoughtful friend. The overthinking is also the part that analyzes a problem thoroughly, that catches the pitfall before it opens underfoot, and that understands nuance in places other people rush past. The sensitivity is the reason I can write at all, can feel, and can understand someone else's pain from the inside—it's the door empathy walks through. And the need for approval is also, underneath its uglier name, the part of me that wants to contribute, to belong, to be seen and loved—the same part that shows up for people and works hard at the relationships that matter. The demons and the angels, it turns out, are made of the same clay.

This is the lesson nature keeps handing back to me if I'm paying attention. The sugarcane is sweet but bears no fruit. The gold shines but carries no fragrance. The lotus is beautiful but only grows in mud. The sun gives light without coolness; the moon gives coolness without warmth. Every single thing in nature is incomplete by design—not a flaw, not an oversight, but a deliberate architecture. You are made the same way. Your weaknesses aren't errors in the programming. They're the price of the strengths sitting right beside them. The same sensitivity that makes you artistic makes you cry at commercials. The same passion that drives you forward burns you out sometimes. The same depth that lets you understand other people also makes you prone to melancholy.

There was a period when I was obsessed with fixing myself—every book, every guru, every technique. I wanted to be confident but not arrogant, ambitious but not anxious, kind but not a pushover, successful but not consumed by work, calm but never boring, and deep without tipping into depressive. I was trying to become something that doesn't exist: a human being with no trade-offs. And what actually happened was that I started to shrink. I muted parts of myself until I became someone pleasant but not entirely real—polishing the surface while the depths underneath went quiet.

One night, exhausted from trying to be everything at once, The One Who Sees spoke louder than usual: you are so afraid of your shadows that you are extinguishing your light too. That was the moment I actually understood it. If I kill my demons—the sensitivity, the overthinking, the need for depth, the fear of being misunderstood—I kill my angels along with them: the empathy, the insight, the ability to connect, and the willingness to be vulnerable in the first place. The demon of overthinking and the angel of deep understanding aren't two different things. They're the same part of me, operating at different frequencies.

What I'm learning, slowly, is that when I stop expecting myself to be complete, I can finally be whole. 'Complete' means having everything. 'Whole' means accepting what you actually are—light and shadow held together, neither one apologizing for the other. The sugarcane doesn't apologize for not being a fruit. The lotus doesn't feel ashamed of the mud it grows in. And I don't have to feel broken for having rough edges of my own.

This has changed how I love people, too. I used to fall for someone's light and then get quietly confused by their darkness—admire someone's kindness and later complain they weren't ambitious enough, appreciate someone's strength and later wish they'd be softer. But kill their demons and their angels might die too, exactly the same as mine would. The person who works too much has the same drive that makes them reliable. The person who feels too deeply has the same capacity that makes them present and loving. The person who overthinks has the same mind that makes them careful with your heart. You can't have the angel without the demon. They share a soul.

What The One Who Sees has actually given me isn't the freedom of being perfect—it's the freedom of no longer performing perfection. The freedom to say I am anxious and also capable. I am sensitive and also strong. I am imperfect, and also enough. And to look at someone I love and say, honestly: I see your challenges, and also your gifts. I accept your shadows and also your light. I don't need you to be complete. I just need you to be real.

Now, when I catch myself wanting to eliminate some part of myself or someone else, I pause and ask what I would actually lose if this quality simply disappeared. If I killed the anxiety, would I lose the thoughtfulness too? If I killed someone's intensity, would their passion go with it? If I killed the overthinking, would the insight vanish along with it? The answer is always the same: you can't separate them.

The goal was never to become perfect. The goal is to become whole. Perfection asks you to eliminate what you don't like about yourself; wholeness asks you to integrate what you actually are. Perfection makes you smaller, cutting away piece after piece; wholeness makes you larger, holding everything together at once. Perfection is lonely—no one ever really reaches it. Wholeness is the version everyone is already welcome inside.

Kab samjhoge ye baat? When will you understand this? The One Who Sees still whispers it to me sometimes, and now I find myself whispering it onwards. The day you stop expecting perfection from yourself and from the people you love and start loving what's actually there instead—that's the day. The day you realize your demons were never enemies to be slain, only energies waiting to be befriended. The day you understand that killing your shadows might leave you without enough light left to see by. That's the day you become free.

I still hear her, especially in the moments quiet enough to let her through. You are not a problem to be solved, she says. You are a mystery to be lived. Your edge is your gift. Your rough spots are your texture. Your missing pieces are your humanity. The same nature that made the lotus beautiful also placed it in mud. The mud was never a mistake. It was part of the design. And so are you.

So here's the question I keep coming back to, and the one I'd ask you too: what part of yourself have you been trying to kill that might be carrying your greatest gift?

With love, from my inner voice to yours.
