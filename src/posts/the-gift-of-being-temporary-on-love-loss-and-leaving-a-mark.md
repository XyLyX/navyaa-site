*I am not a permanent person in anyone's chapter. For a long time, I thought that was a tragedy.*

## What is Temporary Love?

It hits you one day, not with a crash, but with a quiet settling — a realization that feels less like a discovery and more like a truth you've always known, finally coming into focus. I am not a permanent person in anyone's chapter.

For a long time, I treated this as a flaw in my design, a recipe for a heart forever destined to be a visitor rather than a resident. But I've come to define it differently, and the definition matters, because it changes everything about how the loving actually happens. Temporary love isn't love with an asterisk, love that holds something back because it already knows the ending. It's love given in full, on purpose, by someone who has made peace with the fact that "full" and "forever" were never actually the same promise. If impermanence is the role I've been given, then the mission isn't to resent it — it's to become the most memorable, profound, and beautiful temporary person anyone has ever met.

## The Beauty of Being a Visitor in Others' Lives

There's a particular kind of beauty in being a visitor rather than a resident, though it took me a long time to see it as beauty rather than exile. It's a commitment to a love that doesn't keep score. I stay longer than I probably should — not out of obligation or blind hope that staying will somehow convert into permanence, but because when I see a flicker of light in someone, I want to fan it until it becomes a steady flame, regardless of whether I'm the one who eventually gets to stand near that flame forever.

I love harder than what comes back to me, and I want to be clear that this isn't martyrdom dressed up as generosity. It's a belief that love, in its purest form, is an offering rather than a transaction — that pouring kindness, attention, and genuine care into someone doesn't require a guaranteed return to be worth doing, because the person receiving it deserves, in that moment, to feel fully seen and valued regardless of what happens next. I give pieces of myself knowing I won't get them back in the same form — stories, vulnerabilities, insights, support, the fragments of a soul pressed into the palms of the people I meet. I don't give in order to be replenished. I give because those pieces might one day be the cornerstones someone else uses to build something sturdier for themselves, long after I'm no longer the one standing beside them.

## Understanding the Impact of Temporary Relationships

The impact of a relationship was never actually measured by its length, though we're taught, almost by default, to assume it is — that the deepest connections are necessarily the longest ones, and that anything shorter must have been lesser by comparison. I don't think that's true, and I think the seasons make the better metaphor than the calendar does. A season isn't judged a failure for ending; it's understood, correctly, to have done exactly what it was there to do.

I think of myself, in this frame, as the autumn that taught someone to let go with grace — not because autumn stays forever, but because the letting go it teaches wouldn't exist without the ending built into it. The summer that showed someone their own warmth reflected back at a moment they'd forgotten they had it. The spring that convinced someone to bloom again after a long winter they'd started to believe was permanent. None of these seasons need to last a lifetime to leave something that does. The impact of a temporary relationship isn't measured in duration — it's measured in what it illuminated, mended, taught, or simply proved was possible, while it was here.

## How to Embrace Temporary Love

Embracing this kind of love, rather than merely surviving it, asks for a few real, practiced shifts — not abstractions, but things you can actually do the next time you find yourself loving someone whose timeline with you might not stretch to forever.

Love at full volume anyway. The instinct, once you suspect an ending, is to quietly ration what you give — less vulnerability, less time, less of the parts of yourself that are expensive to hand over. Resist that instinct deliberately. A love rationed in anticipation of its ending isn't protected, it's just smaller than it could have been, and smaller doesn't actually hurt less when the ending arrives regardless.

Give without a running tally. Notice when you start keeping score — of who initiated, who gave more, who's owed something — and treat that noticing as information rather than shame. The tally is usually a sign that the giving has quietly shifted from offering into expectation, and expectation is what turns an ending into betrayal instead of simply an ending.

Let the leaving, when it comes, be about listening rather than failure. If you sense the season closing, resist the urge to force it to stay past its natural shape out of fear or attachment. Ask instead what the leaving is actually asking of you — a lesson delivered, a wound tended, a permission given — and let that be enough of an answer, even when it doesn't feel like enough in the moment.

Practice being remembered rather than needing to be kept. This is the hardest shift, and also the most freeing one: measuring the relationship's worth by what it left behind in someone, rather than by whether you're still standing there to witness it.

## Finding Freedom in Letting Go

There's an ache, undeniably, in not being someone's finale — in loving fully and still watching the story continue on without you as one of its later chapters. I don't want to pretend that ache isn't real, or that the reframe I'm describing erases it. It doesn't. But alongside the ache, there's also a genuine freedom in letting go of the need to be kept, one that's easy to underestimate until you've actually felt it.

Needing to be someone's permanent chapter puts a particular kind of pressure on love — a pressure to perform permanence, to prove worth through duration, to treat every ending as evidence of some deficiency in what was given. Letting go of that need doesn't mean loving less. It means the love stops being audition and starts being offering, freely given because it's true in the moment, not because it's angling for a renewal. That shift is where the freedom actually lives — not in caring less about the outcome, but in no longer needing to control it in order to feel like the loving mattered.

## Reflections on Love and Memory

What temporary love leaves behind isn't nothing, even when it feels that way in the immediate aftermath of an ending. It's a paragraph someone dog-ears in their own book — not the whole story, but a page they return to on their own, without being asked to. It's a memory that visits on an ordinary Tuesday, unprompted, and produces a small, involuntary smile before the person even remembers exactly why.

Memory, in this sense, is its own kind of permanence — quieter than staying, but real in a way that outlasts the relationship itself. Proof that some people were never meant to be a whole chapter in someone's story, but were, instead, the highlight of one. That distinction matters. A highlight isn't a lesser role. It's often the part of the book someone actually rereads.

## Conclusion: Celebrating Temporary Connections

So if our paths cross, know this: I won't half-love you. I won't guard my heart in anticipation of an expiration date I can't actually control anyway. I will love you fully, fiercely, and with my whole being for as long as our timelines happen to overlap.

And when the trajectory of our lives gently pulls us apart, don't think of it as an ending because you lost me. Think of it as it truly is — you gained a part of me forever, in whatever it was that autumn, summer, or spring left behind in you. I am a temporary gift. And I am still learning, one season at a time, to wrap myself accordingly.
