*Be practical. I've heard it my whole life, delivered gently, like a life raft I was about to refuse.*

## Is Practicality Sabotaging Your Dreams?

There's a piece of advice I've heard my whole life, and I've recently started to wonder if it's been quietly sabotaging me. Be practical. It came from everywhere growing up—parents, teachers, career counselors, and even friends who meant well. It was always delivered with that same tone: gentle, concerned, and slightly exasperated, as if practicality were a life raft and I was about to jump into open water without one.

The question worth asking isn't whether practicality is bad—obviously it isn't, not categorically. The question is whether it's been doing a second job, quietly, underneath its stated one. Advertised as protection, it can just as easily function as a ceiling, installed early enough in a life that you stop noticing it's there at all. That's the suspicion this essay sits with: not that practical advice is wrong, but that somewhere along the way, for a lot of us, it stopped being a tool and started being a verdict, delivered on every idea before the idea had a chance to make its case.

## The Weight of Practical Advice

For a long time, I listened to it without question. I chose the sensible degree. I took the stable job. I saved the money. I said no to the things that didn't have a clear return on investment. I told myself I was being smart, and honestly, sometimes I was—practicality earns its reputation fairly often. But somewhere along the way, I started to feel like I was living someone else's version of my life, a smaller version that fit neatly into a spreadsheet but didn't quite fit me.

The weight of that advice showed up most clearly in a specific conversation with a friend a few years back. I was telling her about an idea—something creative, something that would have required leaving a stable job and taking a real risk. I'd been thinking about it for months. I'd done the math. I'd made the lists. I'd calculated exactly how long I could survive on savings. And then I heard myself say, "But realistically, it's just not practical." She nodded. She agreed. We both moved on. The idea died right there on the table, killed by a single word I'd used to describe it, before it had even been given a chance to be tried and fail on its own terms.

What I didn't realize at the time was that "practical" had become a shield. It protected me from failure, yes, but it also protected me from trying, from risking, from the possibility that something extraordinary might happen if I let myself want something without a guaranteed outcome attached. Then I heard a clip that named the weight I'd been carrying more precisely than I ever had myself:

> One advice that nobody should follow is be practical. Don't be practical. Nobody, no one in life has ever achieved something significant by being practical. They've all been delusional, they've all been dreamy, they all saw something which wasn't possible, which wasn't practical, and that's why they were able to achieve it. They were never practical in their life.

My first reaction was resistance—that's an absurd overstatement, I thought. Plenty of people achieve significant things by being practical. But I couldn't stop turning it over, because underneath the hyperbole, there was something uncomfortably true: practical thinking keeps you safe, but safety was never the same thing as significance.

## Real Stories of Dreamers

I've been lucky enough to know a few people who ignored the advice to be practical, and none of them were celebrities or billionaires—just ordinary people who decided the conventional path wasn't for them. My college roommate dropped out of a pre-med track to start a tiny bakery in a town that already had three. Everyone told her it was impractical, with too much competition and too small a market. She did it anyway. She isn't rich now, but she's the happiest person I know. She wakes up every day and makes bread for people who love it. That's significant, even if it never shows up on a balance sheet.

A former colleague left a six-figure consulting job to write a novel. He collected rejections for years. Everyone, myself included, privately thought he was being irresponsible. His book came out last spring—it didn't hit any bestseller list, but it got reviewed in a major paper, and he told me that holding that physical copy in his hands was worth every uncertain month it cost him. And then there's my own father, who at forty-seven quit a job he'd held for twenty years to start a small landscaping business with no experience, no clients, and no safety net. My mother was terrified. My grandparents thought he'd lost his mind. But he'd been dreaming about working for himself since he was a teenager, and now, at seventy, he's still working, still his own boss. He told me once, "If I'd waited until it was practical, I'd have waited forever." None of these people were delusional in any clinical sense. They were all just slightly irrational, betting on something the practical version of themselves would have dismissed outright.

## The Balance of Dreamy and Practical

Here's where I have to push back on the quote, though, because as much as it moved me, it isn't the whole truth. I've also watched people undo their lives by rejecting practicality entirely—friends who burned through savings on businesses that never had a real chance, people who chased a dream without a plan, without a safety net, without any willingness to adapt when reality pushed back. Those stories rarely get told, because failure is less inspiring than success, but they're just as real. The speaker was committing a classic error: survivorship bias. We hear about the dreamers who made it. We don't hear about the equally delusional ones whose dreams collapsed and took their families down with them.

There's another problem, too—the quote sets up a false choice, as if be practical" and "be dreamy" were the only two settings available. In reality, the most successful people I know, the ones who built things that actually lasted, were both at once. They had an impossible vision and a spreadsheet. They chased something that looked crazy and then executed it with discipline. They let themselves be delusional about the goal and ruthlessly practical about the method. My roommate, the baker, was dreamy about owning a bakery and extremely practical about her margins, her location, and her suppliers. My father, the landscaper, had a vision of working for himself and a grounded, specific plan for how to land his first ten clients. The balance isn't a compromise between the two modes—it's closer to a division of labor, dreaming of setting the destination and practicality handling the route.

## Finding Your Own Path

Finding your own version of that balance starts with noticing where "practical" actually shows up first in your own thinking. For a lot of us, it arrives before the idea has even finished forming—a reflex rather than a considered judgment, closer to flinching than to evaluating. The first useful step is simply catching that reflex in the act: noticing the moment you reach for "that's not practical" and asking, honestly, whether you're describing the idea's actual odds or just naming your own fear out loud.

From there, it helps to separate the two jobs practicality is supposed to do. One job is deciding what's worth wanting—and practicality is a poor judge of that, because almost nothing worth wanting looks practical at the exact moment you first want it. The other job is figuring out how to get it once you've decided it's worth pursuing—and there, practicality is not just useful but essential. Finding your own path usually means letting the wanting stay unpracticed and undefended for a while, resisting the urge to defend an idea's feasibility before you've even let yourself feel how much you want it, and only bringing in the spreadsheets and the risk assessments once the wanting itself has been allowed to exist without apology.

## A New Perspective on Practicality

Practicality is a tool, not an identity. It's useful when you're executing, planning, managing risk, or building something step by step once a direction has already been chosen. But it's poison when it's the first thing you reach for—when it becomes the lens through which you evaluate every possibility before you've even let yourself want it, deciding what's allowed to exist in your imagination before it's been given a fair hearing.

The speaker in that clip was wrong about a lot. He overgeneralized; he ignored the base rate of failure; he set up a false dichotomy that doesn't survive contact with how real, lasting things actually get built. But he was right about one thing worth holding onto: the world's most significant achievements have rarely come from people who only ever did what was practical. They came from people who saw something that wasn't there yet, and then, against all sensible advice, refused to look away from it until it existed.

## Embracing Both Sides of the Coin

So I'm not going to stand here and tell anyone not to be practical—that advice is too simple and dangerous if taken literally. But I will say this: don't let "be practical" become the reason you never try anything. Don't let it be the voice that kills your ideas before they've had a chance to breathe, or the excuse you reach for when you're really just scared—the thing you say when what you actually mean is I'm afraid this won't work, and I'm afraid of what that will say about me.

What dream have you killed lately with the word practical? What idea have you dismissed not because it was impossible, but because it wasn't safe? What would you do if you let yourself be a little less practical and a little more delusional, just for a moment—not recklessly, but on purpose, with both sides of the coin finally allowed in the same hand? You don't have to quit your job tomorrow. You don't have to bet your savings on a wild idea. But it might be worth noticing when "be practical" has become the first sentence you say to yourself the moment something excites you. Because the most practical thing you can do with your one life might be to let yourself dream a little bigger than the world says you should. And that isn't impractical at all. That's just honest.
