I used to think I was doing fine.

Every morning the words arrived before my eyes opened: *You're doing fine.* A mantra I'd inherited from no one in particular, from the collective hum of people moving with the same mechanical certainty. I believed it completely. Belief was never the problem. The question was who had written it, and why I had never checked.

### The Architecture of Numbness

A life that looks correct from the outside doesn't hurt, and that is exactly what makes it dangerous. There is no rupture, no single moment you can point to and say, "There, *that's where I lost myself."* There is only the alarm, the coffee, the door closing behind you, and meetings blurring like stations on a train you stopped noticing years ago.

I built a life out of checkboxes, and I was so busy ticking them that I never asked who drew the list.

Earning. Paying. Maintaining. Surviving.

Everyone approved. My family relaxed. My friends, equally tired, saw their own resignation reflected in mine and called it *adulthood*, as if the word could carry everything we had quietly stopped asking.

Beneath the noise, though, a question kept surfacing, small and almost inaudible: *What am I actually building?*

I swatted it away for months, maybe years, because answering honestly meant stopping. And stopping was frightening. It meant seeing the distance between the life I was living and the life I *assumed* I was living. It meant feeling something other than tired.

### The Mirror I Couldn't Unsee

Then came a video. A man with a calm voice, not angry, not preachy, just clear, asked questions that dropped into my chest like stones into still water.

*What kind of life is this? At the end of the month, what did you really get? Years will pass. Your hair will turn white. One day death will stand in front of you and ask, "So many years were given to you?" Did you live?*

I wanted to say *that's not me.* The words wouldn't come, because the description wasn't a stranger's. It was mine, stripped of the comfortable language I'd wrapped around it. Not a career, just work. Not a home, just a place I returned to, too exhausted to inhabit. Not living, just the machinery of continuation.

And I had no one to blame. No villain, no catastrophe. Only a thousand small, reasonable choices that had quietly added up to a stranger's life.

That was the real horror. Nothing had been taken from me. I had *handed it over*, a little at a time, and called it responsibility.

### What the Silence Revealed

I sat with it. I didn't rush to fix it, didn't distract myself, and didn't reach for someone's reassurance. I let myself be seen by myself.

In that silence I heard what I'd been drowning out for years.

*Why do you do what you do, from morning to evening?*

The honest answer was humiliating in its smallness: *to keep everything running.* To keep the body fed and the bills paid and the household afloat. Not to build, not to create, not to become. Only to keep the machine from breaking down.

*If that is the only answer, is it enough?*

I wanted to say yes. I wanted survival to be noble. But the question would not let me off. Enough *for what*? Enough to justify the years? Enough to meet death without flinching?

And underneath it, a quieter one: *Who is the "I" that is so busy maintaining, and what does he want when no one is watching?*

I had no answer. Strangely, that not-knowing was the most honest thing I had felt in years.

### Guilt Is a Disguise

It would be easy to spiral here, to mourn the "wasted" years and turn this into one more failure on the list. But guilt is cheap. It *feels* like change while demanding none. It lets us suffer instead of choosing.

What I wanted was not guilt but clarity.

There is a difference between existing and living. Existing is automatic. It needs only momentum, and you can do it for decades without ever making one conscious decision about what your life is *for*. Living requires stopping, asking, choosing, and then choosing again tomorrow, against the pull of everything that would lull you back to sleep.

I had been existing and calling it living because I'd never learned to tell them apart.

### The Shape of the Search

So what *is* the purpose of life?

I don't know, and I'm done pretending otherwise. But searching has taught me what it is not.

It is not the steady accumulation of comfort while the inner life starves.
It is not the quiet pride that *I managed everything*, without ever asking whether everything deserved managing.
It is not a future version of me who finally begins, some mythical one *when things settle down* that never arrives.

Purpose, as I'm beginning to feel it, is closer to awareness. Presence. Waking up *inside* your own life instead of sleepwalking through it. Spending this short, improbable, unrepeatable stretch of consciousness on purpose. It lives in better questions and in the refusal to accept shallow answers just because they're comfortable. It means making something real, offering something true, connecting where it matters, and growing toward a shape you can't fully see yet but can feel pulling you forward.

I don't know what form it will take. But the direction has changed, and that single degree of difference feels like everything.

### The Weight of Time

They say life is short. That isn't quite right.

Life is exactly as long as it is. We simply treat it as longer. We file the important things under *someday*, as though someday were a place with an address.

There is no someday. There is only now, already happening, whether or not I'm present for it. This wasn't a crisis or a tragedy or a rock-bottom moment. It was the quiet, devastating recognition that time has always been moving, and I had been letting it run through my hands like water.

But a wake-up call doesn't have to be a single moment. It can be a practice, a daily returning, or a decision to stay awake even when every part of me wants to drift back into autopilot.

### High Time

I don't want death to look at me one day and smile in pity.

I want to meet that moment, whenever it comes, knowing I tried. That I questioned. That I adjusted. That I refused to let the years blur into routine and call it a life.

The video's questions are mine now. They work like a compass, pointing not to a destination but reminding me to keep checking my direction.

And for the first time in a long while, I feel the weight of my own life. Not as a burden, not as pressure, but as something else:

**Responsibility.**

Not the duty to succeed or to meet anyone's expectations. The duty to *be here*. To show up for my own existence.

Not someday.
Not when things calm down.
Not when I have more money, more time, and more clarity.

Now.

I used to think I was doing fine.

I'm not fine.

I'm awake.

And that is where everything begins.
